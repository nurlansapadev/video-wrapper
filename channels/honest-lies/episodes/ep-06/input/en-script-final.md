## BEAT 1. HOOK

The island of Taiwan, once a province of imperial China. Its capital, Taipei. A February morning with a pleasant, cool breeze. In a small shop near the train station, seven men sit around a table. Over hot soy milk and sesame flatbread, they're talking about something serious.

Six of them are a Taiwanese cabinet minister and his top officials. The seventh is their guest from America. He runs research labs for RCA — the same company that sold the world its first color TVs.

Fine weather, a simple but very good breakfast, an easy, relaxed mood. Everything goes smoothly.

The American names his price — ten million dollars — and his timeline: four years for the technology Taiwan needs.

The Taiwanese nod. They're in. They finish their soy milk and head out.

The bill for breakfast comes to about ten bucks.

Small, proud Taiwan has a tried-and-true friend — America. In the mid-seventies, with mainland China leaning on it hard, the island was desperate for some guarantee of its own security. And it decided the way to get one was to become indispensable. An island with no oil, no coal, no natural resources to speak of, bet everything on technology.

And America gave it just that.

Let me tell you how it happened.

## BEAT 2. THE OFFICIAL VERSION

> Level reporter. Hook words for beat 6: **"they stole"**, **"technology transfer"**, **"grave concern"**, **"silicon shield"**.

To understand what that breakfast was worth, just reach into your pocket. The processor in your iPhone was made in Taiwan. So were the Nvidia chips they're using right now to train artificial intelligence. Out of every ten dollars that Apple, Nvidia and the rest of corporate America pay to get their chips made, seven go to one Taiwanese company — TSMC. An island the size of Maryland makes the most complicated things human beings know how to make.

So how did that happen? You usually hear one of two stories.

The first is the genius story. A visionary named Morris Chang came up with a brilliant business model, and everything else fell into place. It's a nice fairy tale for the cover of a business magazine, and there's some truth in it. But it leaves out the best part.

The second story is a lot harsher. This spring, on Fox News, the President of the United States put it bluntly: *"They stole our chip industry."* And it's not just campaign talk. Politicians in both parties will tell you that America once made close to forty percent of the world's chips — and today it makes barely a tenth. Do the math, and something really did go missing. So somebody must have taken it.

Genius or thief? To decide, we have to go back to that table — to the morning of February 7th, 1974 — and look at the spot Taiwan was in.

It was a bad spot. In the fall of 1971, Taiwan was thrown out of the United Nations; China's seat went to Beijing. A few months later, Nixon flew to Beijing and shook Mao's hand. One country after another moved its embassy from Taipei to the mainland. And less than a hundred miles across the strait, the government of an enormous country called the island its province and promised to take it back someday.

Taiwan had an army, but it was nothing next to its giant neighbor's. And Taipei had just learned the hard way what political promises are worth: one day you've got a seat on the UN Security Council, the next you're crossed off every list. So they made a gutsy call. Don't beg for protection. Make yourself worth protecting.

## BEAT 3. THE TURN

> The break.

So forget the visionaries and the thieves. Neither kind was at that table. There was an American engineer offering the island American technology. And two years later, Taiwan would buy it — officially, down to the last comma in the contract.

Politicians can holler about theft all they want, but out in the real world, one thing tells a theft from a deal. A receipt.

This deal has one. The ten million was the budget for the whole plan. The technology itself cost three and a half million dollars, paid on March 5th, 1976.

America itself handed Taiwan the keys to the future. And the whole time, Washington stood right behind the island, watching its back. Let's unwind this from the start.

## BEAT 4. THE REAL STORY

### 4.1. The Breakfast

> Impressed pro. Thread: desperation.

At the head of the table that morning sat the Minister of Economic Affairs, Sun Yun-suan. An engineer by training, he'd built the island's power plants, and he was sure of one thing: a country that can't make complicated things for itself will spend its whole life asking other people for them.

The guest from America was Pan Wen-yuan. Born in China, he'd spent close to thirty years at RCA's labs in New Jersey. And back then, RCA was roughly what Apple is today: televisions, radio, satellites, a whole research empire of its own.

Pan's idea was simple. Taiwan lived on assembly — cheap hands putting together other people's electronics. But that kind of work always moves to wherever the hands are cheaper, and one day it would move out of Taiwan too. A chip is a different animal. A chip is know-how. And you can't load know-how onto a ship and send it off along with the assembly line.

The minister said yes on the spot. And the books from that morning read like a joke on the whole history of big business. The breakfast: under four hundred Taiwan dollars. The plan that would grow into the most important factory on the planet: four hundred million. A million times more, give or take. Maybe the biggest jackpot in history got hit between sips of soy milk.

*Not bad for a breakfast.*

If nobody's afraid of you, there's only one thing left — become so necessary that the world's factories stop without you. Everything Taiwan did next adds up to exactly that. And the first step was America's.

### 4.2. America Gave

> Admiration for the craft. Thread: betting on know-how.

Taiwan shopped for a teacher with a cold eye — fourteen companies made the list. It went with RCA, and what it bought wasn't the hottest technology on the market, not by a long shot.

RCA had thrifty little chips that barely sipped electricity but were considered slow. No good for serious computers — but perfect for the small stuff: digital watches, pocket calculators, musical greeting cards. The big players looked down their noses at them. That's exactly what Taiwan wanted: to slip into the business quietly, through the back door.

Young Taiwanese engineers headed to America to learn — nineteen at first, more later, in New Jersey, Ohio and Florida. Before they left, Minister Sun gathered them together and, as one of them later remembered, told them: "This is the country's first technology plan. Success only. Failure is forbidden."

Ministers make pretty speeches all the time. These guys turned one into silicon. By the end of 1977, eighty-one out of every hundred chips coming off the island's demonstration line were good — better than RCA's own lines back home. The student beat the teacher with the teacher's own technology, in less than two years.

Credit where it's due: RCA sold honestly and taught honestly. Twelve years later, it walked away from chips altogether. The student stayed.

But it was still a long way from watch chips to TSMC. And in between came one night after which Taiwan didn't have an ally left.

### 4.3. The Guarantor

> Cold, then sympathy. Thread: the ally leaves — the bet on being indispensable.

From 1954 on, America and Taiwan were bound by a mutual defense treaty. In plain English: lay a finger on the island, and you deal with the Seventh Fleet.

Then, one night in December 1978, the American ambassador drove out to wake up Taiwan's president, Chiang Ching-kuo. In seven hours, he told him, Washington would announce that it recognized Beijing — and the treaty protecting the island was being torn up. Seven hours' notice that the shield was gone.

To be fair, in the spring of 1979, Congress passed the Taiwan Relations Act. The island would still be sold defensive weapons, and the United States would consider any attempt to settle Taiwan's future by force a matter of *"grave concern."* So the umbrella came back — with holes in it. Nowhere in that law does America promise to fight for the island.

That's when the idea from the snack shop became a plan. And a plan needed a man. Funny thing — America had trained him, too.

Morris Chang spent twenty-five years at Texas Instruments and worked his way up to running its entire semiconductor business. They never gave him the top job. He later said he understood why: in that corporate culture, an Asian man wasn't going to make it all the way up. In 1985, Taiwan invited him over and, more or less, handed him a blank check.

### 4.4. The Receipt America Never Signed

> Impressed pro, then deadpan. Peak reveal.

In American popular culture, Morris Chang doesn't exist. He's no Steve Jobs. If the Silicon Valley of those years was a glittering casino, Chang wasn't the face on the billboard. He was the grim mechanic down in the basement who knew how the roulette wheels actually worked.

His idea sounded strange for its time. Don't make your own chips — make other people's. You design a chip out in California, we print it. And we swear we'll never compete with you.

For that he needed money and partners, so he went to the people he knew personally: Intel, Texas Instruments, Motorola, AMD, Sony. Every last one of them said no. Only Philips, from Holland, said yes — money and technology for about a quarter of the company. Roughly half came from Taiwan's government. In February 1987, TSMC opened its doors.

So there it is — the receipt America never signed. A stake in what would become the most important factory on the planet was offered to American companies in person, eye to eye. The answer was polite: thanks, but no thanks.

A few years later, AMD's founder, Jerry Sanders, came out with a line people would quote for decades: *"Real men have fabs."* Real men own their factories. It sounded proud. But a modern chip factory costs billions and goes obsolete in a couple of years, and one American company after another did the math. Why build, when you can just design and farm out the printing?

From early on, Nvidia designed its chips without a factory of its own. Apple moved its iPhone processors to Taiwan. And AMD itself sold off its factories in 2009. Turns out real men like outsourcing, too. Every one of those orders went to the place that promised to print well and never compete — a promise TSMC is still keeping.

And that's the answer to why it's this island. America made it the leader twice. First it sold Taiwan the technology and trained its engineers. Then it brought Taiwan the orders, because that was cheaper. And the whole time, America held an umbrella over the island so it could build in peace.

Nobody stole anything. An island nobody was afraid of simply became the one place nobody can do without.

## BEAT 5. THE FALLOUT

> Deadpan skeptic. The "so what?" anchor.

In 1990, America had more than a third of the world's chipmaking capacity. By 2020, twelve percent. Nobody snuck that warehouse out at night. We set it out on the curb ourselves and thanked them for hauling it away.

And the island got what those seven men had in mind over breakfast. Phones, cars, the servers that run artificial intelligence — none of it works without Taiwan.

But being indispensable has a flip side: everybody wants the indispensable thing for themselves. In 2022, Chen Wenling, an economist at a government think tank in Beijing, told a conference that if the West ever hit China with Russia-style sanctions, Taiwan would have to be taken back — and TSMC seized.

Our side's answer wasn't any softer. Congressman Seth Moulton proposed making it crystal clear to Beijing: if they invade, *"we will destroy TSMC."* An American military journal even ran a paper on the idea, titled "Broken Nest." Taiwan's defense minister kept his answer short: we won't allow it. The island made itself indispensable so it would be left alone — and now both sides of the strait talk about it like a trophy.

There's a third option, too: move it. Under pressure from Washington, TSMC is building plants in Arizona. Morris Chang, now in his nineties, says making chips in America costs about half again as much. So now we want back the very thing we once turned down — and we're ready to overpay for it.

## BEAT 6. THE TRANSLATION

> Deadpan, cold clarity. No new information.

Let's strip the political packaging off this story and look at the paperwork.

*"They stole our chip industry."* America sold that technology on its own, signed and stamped — and passed when it was offered a stake. There's no such thing as theft with a receipt.

*"Technology transfer."* A fancy name for a warehouse clearance sale. America shipped out what it considered slow-moving stock — circuits for wristwatches. It's not the buyer's fault that it turned marked-down goods into something close to a monopoly.

*"Grave concern."* In the language of diplomacy, that's a complaint nobody is obliged to act on. Translated: "we'll send an angry fax" — not "we'll send aircraft carriers." Taiwan has been living in the gap between those two sentences for more than forty years.

*"Silicon shield."* A journalist came up with that term in 2001, when it was all over but the shouting — the strategy got its name after it had already worked. On the island itself, they put it more simply. They call TSMC "the sacred mountain that protects the nation."

But in 1974 there was no shield and no mountain. Just a little table in a shop by the train station, seven men, and a four-year plan.

## BEAT 7. THE FINALE

> Arny without the mask. Dry warmth. No end-of-video markers.

This spring, the President of the United States walked out of a meeting with China's leader and called Taiwan *"a very good negotiating chip."* Chip. Looks like he didn't even catch the pun.

And in Taipei, around the same time, they put up a plaque where that little shop used to stand. It was torn down long ago — all that's left is the alley. The mayor came in person and called that breakfast the best-value meal in the country's history. Hard to argue with him. Out of a conversation over soy milk grew a company that today is worth two trillion dollars.

The world economy remembers thousands of contracts signed with gold pens under the flashbulbs. Most of that paper isn't worth the ink it was signed with. The real reshaping of the world happened at a table in a snack shop by the train station.

That deal was made by people who had nobody to scare and nowhere to retreat — across the strait sat an enormous China that considered the island its own, and their old friends were looking the other way. They understood the main thing: nobody was coming to protect them. So they stopped waiting. They decided not to beg the world order for mercy, but to make themselves essential to it. To make sure that all of humanity could no longer get by without their little island.

Today, ten dollars in New York gets you a coffee and a muffin, handed over without a second look. Back then, in Taipei, a pocket-change tab for morning soy milk became the price the island paid for its right to exist.

The check from that breakfast didn't survive. But what it bought is probably sitting in your pocket right now.
