# Script (EN) — The FBI Said He Saved Rajiv Gandhi. A Year Later, He Plotted Car Bombs.

---

## BEAT 1. HOOK

Washington. FBI headquarters on Pennsylvania Avenue. Director William Webster makes an announcement: the Bureau has thwarted a plot to assassinate the Prime Minister of India, Rajiv Gandhi — on American soil, during his visit with President Reagan. A few days earlier in New Orleans, FBI agents picked up a group of Sikh radicals right outside a hotel entrance.

The man who led the FBI to them ran a war school in the Alabama woods. The FBI said he saved Rajiv Gandhi's life. A year later, he'd be the one led away in handcuffs — for plotting to blow up two cars.

So whose side was he really on?

You're about to see how a three-hundred-fifty-dollar course became an FBI trap, a terror headline and a prison cell — and why two federal courts couldn't agree whether the plot was even a federal case.

---

## BEAT 2. THE OFFICIAL VERSION

Before we get to whose side this man was on, let's hear how the FBI itself told the story that spring. It's a short version, and everything in it is right where it should be.

On May 13th, 1985, FBI Director William Webster said the Bureau had thwarted a plot by Sikh radicals to kill Rajiv Gandhi during his visit to the United States. The court papers went bigger. The goal, they said, was the "revolutionary overthrow" of the government of India. And Webster added a line fit to be carved in granite: "We won't allow our successes to blind us to the potential for terrorist activity in this country."

To see why this was so serious, you need about thirty seconds of history. In the summer of 1984, the Indian Army stormed the Golden Temple in Amritsar, the holiest shrine of the Sikh faith, where armed separatists had dug in. Four months later, Indira Gandhi was shot dead by her own Sikh bodyguards.

Her son Rajiv became prime minister. Sikh radicals wanted a state of their own in Punjab, and they considered his family enemy number one.

Now look at it from Washington. In June 1985, Rajiv Gandhi is flying in to see Reagan on his first state visit. South Lawn, military band, dinner with the Reagans. The administration wants to sell India technology and weapons and, while it's at it, ease the country gently away from Moscow — just that May, the two sides had signed off on the rules for transferring American technology. If the guest gets shot on American soil, all of that diplomacy goes into the ground with him.

And the FBI saved it. A clean undercover operation, arrests before the visit, not a shot fired. It reads like a chapter out of a counterintelligence textbook.

Only the press release left out one detail. Who came up with the whole thing.

---

## BEAT 3. THE TURN

It wasn't the FBI that set the trap. The credit goes to a Vietnam veteran who ran a mercenary school in the Alabama woods. He charged every student three hundred fifty dollars for a two-week course.

Stop and think about that for a second. In the mid-eighties, right here in the United States, a school of war was operating out in the open. It advertised in a magazine and printed its price in the ad. You paid, you drove out to the woods, and for two weeks you learned to shoot live ammunition and fight like a commando. Like the karate classes my son took as a teenager.

The veteran said all of it was perfectly legal.

The Sikhs found the camp through that magazine ad. The owner turned his students over to the feds, and the feds walked in on a ready-made case.

So the FBI didn't hunt anybody down. All that high-stakes geopolitics grew out of a strange little business that, by its owner's account, broke no laws. Keep that ad in mind. Before this story is over, the man who placed it will be called a hero, a businessman and a criminal — and every one of those labels will stick. And to get a sense of how big that business was, all you had to do was open one popular magazine.

---

## BEAT 4. THE REAL STORY

### 4.1. The School

In the eighties, a certain kind of American man had his own magazine — *Soldier of Fortune*. At its peak it sold around a hundred and fifty thousand copies, and the back pages carried the kind of ads you wouldn't find in your local paper. Tactical manuals, gear, and every now and then an offer of services nobody asks about out loud. That's where the school from Alabama advertised, and it didn't hide what it was. It was called the Mercenary School.

The owner's name was Frank Camper. In Vietnam he'd served in long-range reconnaissance — small teams that went out past the front lines and stayed in the jungle for days at a time. In 1980 he opened his school on the banks of the Warrior River, in a wooded county outside Birmingham. Besides shooting, students learned to survive and to move through the woods without being found.

The business model was smarter than it looked. Camper sold the same product twice. First to the students, for three hundred fifty dollars. Then to the government. By his own account, he'd started working for the FBI back in 1970. And information on the people who passed through his woods quietly made its way to the authorities. He described it without a shred of modesty: "I invented it. I opened it. From that day forward we were part of an intelligence operation."

The arrangement ran quietly and kept everyone happy. Until late 1984.

### 4.2. The Deal

In the fall of 1984, a small group of Sikhs showed up in Alabama. Cecil Moses, who ran the FBI's Birmingham office, later put it plainly: "They started shopping around for guns." And they showed an interest in mercenary training. According to Camper, during the course they asked about time bombs and armored cars — odd questions for a firing range in the woods.

Camper told the authorities. Birmingham opened an undercover operation, and men looking for weapons needed someone to sell them weapons. That part went to an FBI agent named Thomas Norris.

Thomas Norris was a former Navy SEAL. In the spring of 1972 in Vietnam, he and a single South Vietnamese partner made their way through enemy positions and brought out two downed American airmen. For that, he received the Medal of Honor. Six months later, a bullet hit him in the head. He lost an eye and part of his skull, and the Navy retired him for his wounds. In 1979, Norris walked into the FBI and asked them to overlook his disability. Director William Webster gave him this answer: "If you can pass the same test as anybody else applying for this organization, I will waive your disabilities." Norris passed the same tests as everyone else.

So picture it: a one-eyed Medal of Honor recipient playing an arms dealer for men who'd trained at a mercenary school in Alabama. The curtain was up, and everyone in the room had a part to play. The lead actor's whole job was to get the customer to say too much.

And the customers did. In early April, the group got to the real order. On their own initiative, they asked their new supplier to help arrange the assassination of Rajiv Gandhi during his upcoming visit to the States. And Gandhi wasn't the only target.

They said it out loud. To a federal agent.

There was no reason to wait any longer. The FBI moved in.

On May 4th, 1985, in New Orleans, agents arrested the plotters outside the hotel where another name on their list was staying — Bhajan Lal, the chief minister of the Indian state of Haryana. He'd come to America on a strictly private matter: to see his eye doctor.

Nine days later, William Webster — the same director who'd once waived a missing eye — announced the Bureau's success.

Clean work. Except that two names were missing from the list of arrests. The FBI had its headline. The question of whose side its star informant was really on would have to wait.

### 4.3. Two Got Away

And the man in Alabama who'd trained them was about to find out what "legal" is worth in a newspaper. Before that, though — the two who got away. Their names were Lal Singh and Ammand Singh, and both lived in Queens. In New York they were charged with conspiring against Gandhi, but nobody got to them in time. The FBI believed they'd slipped into Canada.

On June 12th, Rajiv Gandhi stood on the South Lawn of the White House next to Reagan. The visit went off without a hitch. The FBI had defended its turf beautifully.

On June 23rd, 1985, at Tokyo's Narita Airport, a suitcase exploded while baggage was being unloaded from a Canadian flight. Two baggage handlers were killed. Less than an hour later, Air India Flight 182 blew apart in midair off the coast of Ireland. Three hundred twenty-nine people died.

It took the newspapers only a few days to put two and two together. Lal Singh was named a suspect in the bombing. According to Canada's *Globe and Mail*, twenty-five-year-old Lal Singh had flown one leg of that chain of flights, and thirty-three-year-old Ammand Singh had been in Toronto just before the disaster. Indian officials added just one more detail: Lal Singh had trained at that very school in Alabama.

In late July, India's foreign minister stood up in Parliament and demanded that the United States either shut down schools like that or outlaw them. "It is unfortunate," he said, "if the United States took an extreme legalistic view on the issue."

Camper answered from Alabama: "Everything we do here is legal. I'm not responsible for them after they leave the school." And he added: "We do not train terrorists."

Camper called his business legal and considered the matter closed. Lal Singh's name would hang in the air for seven long years. Not until 1992 would a Canadian investigator find him in an Indian police station and sit down across the table from him.

But first, the FBI's trap would end up under investigation itself.

### 4.4. The Peak — A Store

In May 1986, almost exactly a year after New Orleans, the authorities showed up at Camper's house in Hueytown. Only this time they hadn't come for advice. The owner of the school was led away in handcuffs.

The charges sounded like something written for a cheap TV movie. Two women from California, owners of private schools, wanted to get rid of some disgruntled former employees. According to the prosecution, Camper and two instructors from his camp helped them plan to bomb those people's cars. If you believe the prosecution, schools were ordering bombs from a school.

In 1987, Camper was convicted on weapons and conspiracy charges and sentenced to fourteen years. He called the whole thing a frame-up: he'd done too much for the government, he said, and now he was being taken off the board. Years later, a federal prosecutor in Los Angeles answered that in two sentences: "He was not prosecuted because of any desire to silence him. He was prosecuted because of the crimes he committed."

So whose side was Frank Camper on? The court locked him up as a criminal. But in 1988, while he was already behind bars, his work for the government was confirmed in public, before Congress. And Cecil Moses, that same FBI man from Birmingham, wrote a letter supporting his parole: "A guy like Camper is a gold mine to law enforcement."

A criminal to the court. A gold mine to the FBI.

So whose side was he on? His own.

I have no desire whatsoever to judge him. Picture a man who came home from the jungles of Vietnam with skills that hardly anyone needs in peacetime. Camper simply built his own world around them.

The school on the Warrior River was neither a clever government trap nor an international incubator for terrorists. It was his own personal foxhole. He sold what he knew to anyone willing to pay, and he handed the information to the FBI — surviving the only way he knew how, with a perfect set of checks and balances between the radicals and the feds.

The much-celebrated "rescue" of India's prime minister wasn't some calculated master plan. It was the natural result of that hard-edged balancing act. The veteran played by his own rules of survival, and he did what he set out to do.

But once the headlines died down, the government stepped in.

## BEAT 5. THE FALLOUT

And in court, it turned out that a headline-making plot doesn't translate well into the dry language of American law.

According to the case record, the New Orleans charges rested on a statute against conspiring to kill an "official guest" of the United States government. It sounds impressive, but that status comes with a procedure: the Secretary of State designates an official guest, on paper, through the Office of Protocol. Nobody had designated Bhajan Lal. He'd come to see his eye doctor, and the State Department only gave him a security detail after the arrests. A federal judge in New Orleans kept it simple: if he isn't an official guest, there's no federal crime — and he threw out the indictment. The government appealed, and a panel of the appeals court ruled the other way. Which meant the question "was this even a federal crime" came down to a protocol form that nobody had filled out.

Among the five defendants was a New Orleans cab driver named Jatinder Singh Ahluwalia. He spent twenty-three months behind bars waiting for trial. In April 1987, a federal judge acquitted him: there wasn't enough evidence. When he got out, he said: "I feel there is still justice in this country if you stand for your soul." Nearly two years of a man's life — that's what the word "thwarted" cost one man the court couldn't tie to any of it.

Now, Lal Singh. In 1992, he was arrested in India. When they caught him, he tried to swallow a cyanide capsule and bit his tongue so badly that most of it had to be removed. Inspector Jim Cunningham of the Royal Canadian Mounted Police flew out to that Indian police station. He questioned Lal Singh and came to a conclusion he later repeated under oath: "I did not feel personally that he was involved." The airline clerk who had checked those suitcases couldn't pick him out. The bag tagged "L. Singh" hadn't even been sent to Flight 182 — it went onto a flight through Narita. And so a suspicion that had seemed so obvious in the summer of 1985 quietly fell apart.

As for Rajiv Gandhi, he made it through that American spring. On May 21st, 1991, no longer prime minister, he arrived at a campaign rally in the small southern Indian town of Sriperumbudur. A young woman bent down toward his feet as if to bow, and set off a belt of explosives. She had been sent by the Tamil Tigers — over India's role in the war in Sri Lanka. The Sikhs had nothing to do with it.

Stopping that plot in America only bought him time. He was protected from one set of enemies, and six years later, another set got to him.

---

## BEAT 6. THE TRANSLATION — one sentence on trial

Out of everything that was said in this story, I'd keep one sentence. Frank Camper, summer of 1985: "Everything we do here is legal." Let's take it apart, word by word.

"Everything." In the normal world, if you want to open a bank account, you have to prove who you are and fill out a stack of forms. To get into Camper's war course, a magazine ad was enough. So much for "everything."

"We." Who exactly is "we"? The owner of the camp, the FBI that collected files through him, and American justice. One pronoun, three parties — and every one of them was absolutely sure it was the one making the rules.

"Legal." In everyday life, a strong painkiller at the pharmacy takes a prescription with a doctor's signature. Two weeks of shooting live rounds in the Alabama woods took three hundred fifty dollars. That's it. And when it came time to try the plotters, the judges didn't argue over whether these men had wanted to kill someone — they argued over whether the State Department had their target on the books as an official guest.

So Camper wasn't lying. All of it was legal. The law was just looking the other way.

---

## BEAT 7. FINALE

Camper walked out of prison in December 1991, after serving about five and a half years of his fourteen. A few years later he had a small computer company outside Birmingham and an international patent on a cash-handling machine. A veteran who made his name on the art of survival and the trade in secrets ended up inventing a machine that just counts money.

And still, in the spring of 1985, the system worked — the way it's supposed to work in this country. A veteran in Alabama told the right people. A one-eyed Medal of Honor recipient, who'd once been hired by the FBI on the same terms as everyone else, played his part to the end. And the prime minister of a faraway country stood next to Ronald Reagan on the White House lawn and flew home alive. America took good care of its guest.

Forty years later, Washington and New Delhi argue over tariffs and haggle over every percentage point. Better that way. Let them haggle. Tariffs are just money. And money can always be counted.

A life can't.
